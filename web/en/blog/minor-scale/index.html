Minor scale: natural, harmonic and relative

The minor scale gives songs that darker or more nostalgic mood. And it's already hidden inside every major scale.

The natural minor scale formula

T – S – T – T – S – T – T

Starting on A, this formula lands only on white keys:

Compared with the major scale, the natural minor has lowered 3rd, 6th and 7th (♭3, ♭6, ♭7). The ♭3 is the most important: it's what makes the tonic chord minor.

Relative key: C major and A minor are “siblings”

C major and A minor use exactly the same notes, just starting in different places. That's why they're called relative and have the same key signature.

  • To find the relative minor of a major key: go down a step and a half (3 half steps) up from the tonic, or take the VI degree.
  • To find the relative major of a minor key: go up a step and a half.

💡 Tip If the song has the key signature of G major (one ♯) but starts and ends on E minor, it's in E minor. The key signature isn't enough: listen for where the music “rests”.

Parallel key: C major and C minor

Parallel keys share the same tonic but have different scales: C major (C D E F G A B) and C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭). Many songs “borrow” chords from the parallel key, like the Fm in C major: C – F – Fm – C.

Harmonic minor scale

In natural minor, the 5th degree gives a minor chord (Em in A minor), which doesn't pull back to the tonic very strongly. The solution was raise the 7th half step:

Now the V chord becomes E7 (E G♯ B D), which pulls strongly toward Am. The step and a half jump between F and G♯ gives this scale its typical “Arabic” or “gypsy” flavor.

Melodic minor scale

To smooth out that leap, the minor melodic also raises the 6th: A B C D E F♯ G♯ A. In classical music it goes up like this and comes down like the natural minor; in jazz it's used the same both ways.

In practice, songs in minor keys mix things up: they use the natural chords and swap the V for E7 when it's time to go back to the tonic. Classic example: Am – Dm – E7 – Am.

The minor II–V–I progression becomes Bm7(♭5) – E7 – Am, where the half-diminished shows up, explained in seventh chords.

Bonus: the minor pentatonic

Taking the 2nd and the ♭6 out of the natural minor leaves five notes: A C D E G. É a minor pentatonic, the scale most used in rock and blues solos, because it almost never sounds “wrong”.

Quick summary
  • Natural minor: W H W W H W W (♭3, ♭6, ♭7).
  • Relative minor = VI degree of the major (3 half steps down): C ↔ Am.
  • Relative keys share the same key signature.
  • Harmonic minor: raised 7th → V becomes E7 in A minor.
  • Melodic minor: raised 6th and 7th.
  • Minor pentatonic: 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7.

Frequently asked questions

What is the natural minor scale formula?

Whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole (W H W W H W W).

What is the relative minor of C major?

A minor. Both scales use the same notes.

How do you find the relative minor of any key?

Go down a step and a half (three half steps) from the major tonic, or take the 6th degree of the scale. G major → E minor.

What's the difference between natural and harmonic minor?

The harmonic minor raises the 7th by a half step (G♯ in A minor). That turns the V chord into a major chord with a seventh (E7), which resolves more strongly to the tonic.
