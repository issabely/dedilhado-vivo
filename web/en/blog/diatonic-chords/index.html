Diatonic chords: the chords that “go together” in each key

Why do so many songs use C, G, Am and F together? Because these chords come from the same scale. That's diatonic chords (the harmonic field), the tool for playing songs by ear and writing your own.

What diatonic chords (the harmonic field) are

Diatonic chords is the set of chords built using only the notes of a scale. On each degree of the major scale, we apply “skip one, take one” and see what kind of chord shows up.

Diatonic chords in C major (triads)

DegreeIIIIIIIVVVIVII
NotesC E GD F AE G BF A CG B DA C EB D F
ChordCDmEmFGAmB°
Typemajorminorminormajormajorminordiminished

The pattern repeats in any major key:

I · IIm · IIIm · IV · V · VIm · VII°

With sevenths (seventh chords)

Stacking one more third, each degree gets its own natural seventh:

DegreeIIIIIIIVVVIVII
C majorC7MDm7Em7F7MG7Am7Bm7(♭5)
Default7Mm7m77M7m7m7(♭5)

Notice that there's only one dominant seventh chord (7) among the diatonic chords: the one on the V degree. That's why it's called “dominant”.

Functions: home, moving away and tension

Each chord has a role in the song's “story”:

FunctionDegreesFeelingIn C
TonicI (and III, VI)Home, restC (Em, Am)
SubdominantIV (and II)Leaving home, movementF (Dm)
DominantV (and VII)Tension, wants to go backG7 (B°)

Most songs follow the path home → away → tension → home: I – IV – V – I.

The most common progressions

I – V – VIm – IV

C – G – Am – F, with the notes close together, the way a pianist would play it.

In C: C – G – Am – F. In G: G – D – Em – C. It's the progression behind hundreds of pop, sertanejo and rock hits. Start at another point and you get variations: VIm – IV – I – V (Am – F – C – G).

II – V – I

In C: Dm7 – G7 – C7M. It's the most important cadence in jazz and bossa nova: subdominant, dominant and tonic.

Listen for the feeling of “arriving” on the last chord.

I – IV – V

In A: A – D – E. The basis of blues, rock’n’roll and forró.

I – VIm – IIm – V

In C: C – Am – Dm – G. The progression of 1950s ballads and many love songs.

Secondary dominants: the 7 that comes “from outside”

Sometimes a 7 chord shows up that isn't in the key, like A7 in C major. It's the dominant of Dm (the V of II) and it “pushes” the music there: C – A7 – Dm – G7 – C. Rule of thumb: a 7 chord usually goes to the chord a a fifth below (A7 → D, E7 → A, D7 → G).

Using diatonic chords to figure out songs by ear

  1. Find the whole step: it's usually the chord where the music ends and “rests”.
  2. Write the 7 diatonic chords of that key.
  3. Try it first I, IV, V and VIm: they show up in the vast majority of songs.
  4. If none fit, look for secondary dominants or chords borrowed from the minor key.

Quick summary
  • Diatonic chords = chords built only from the notes of the scale.
  • Major pattern: I, IIm, IIIm, IV, V, VIm, VII°.
  • With sevenths: 7M, m7, m7, 7M, 7, m7, m7(♭5).
  • Functions: tonic (I), subdominant (IV), dominant (V).
  • Champion progressions: I–V–VIm–IV and II–V–I.

Frequently asked questions

What are diatonic chords (the harmonic field)?

It's the set of chords you can build using only the notes of a scale. In C major: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and B°.

What are the diatonic chords of G major?

G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em and F♯°. With sevenths: G7M, Am7, Bm7, C7M, D7, Em7 and F♯m7(♭5).

Why is the V degree called the dominant?

Because it's the chord with the most tension in the key and “dominates” the return to the tonic. It's the only one that naturally forms a 7 chord (dominant seventh).

What's the most used progression in pop music?

I – V – VIm – IV. In C: C – G – Am – F.
