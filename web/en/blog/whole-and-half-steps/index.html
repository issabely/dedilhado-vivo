Whole step and half step: music's ruler

Scales, intervals and chords are all measured with the same ruler: the whole step and the half step. Once you get this, the rest of theory is easy.

Half step: the smallest step

O half step is the smallest distance between two notes on the piano and the guitar.

  • In keyboard: it's going from one key to the immediate neighbor, whether white or black.
  • In guitar: it's moving one fret on the same string.

Whole step: two half steps

A whole step equals two half steps. On the keyboard, it's skipping one key. On the guitar, it's moving two frets.

The two natural half steps: E-F and B-C

Look at the keyboard: between almost every pair of white keys there's a black one. The exceptions are E–F e B–C. There's no black key between them, so the distance is only a half step.

FromToDistance
CD1 whole step
DE1 whole step
EF½ step (half step)
FG1 whole step
GA1 whole step
AB1 whole step
BC½ step (half step)

Just memorize this: “E-F and B-C are stuck together”. Everything else is a whole step apart.

💡 Tip That's why, on the guitar, there's no “E sharp” as a separate fret from F: the fret after E is already F.

Sharp, flat and natural

The symbols that change a note's pitch are called accidentals:

SymbolNameWhat it doesExample
♯SharpUp 1 half stepF♯ = black key to the right of F
♭FlatDown 1 half stepB♭ = black key to the left of B
♮NaturalCancels the ♯ or ♭ and returns to the natural noteF♮ = plain F
𝄪Double sharpUp 2 half stepsF𝄪 sounds like G
𝄫Double flatDown 2 half stepsB𝄫 sounds like A

In chord symbols, the sharp appears as # and the flat as b: F# (F sharp), Bb (B flat).

Enharmonics: same sound, two names

F♯ and G♭ are the same key. Notes like this are enharmonic. So why are there two names? Because in each scale we use each letter only once. In the D major scale, for example, the right name is F♯ (not G♭), because G comes right after.

There are also enharmonics with white keys: E♯ = F, B♯ = C, F♭ = E e C♭ = B. They're rare, but they show up in some scales.

The chromatic scale

Playing all 12 notes in a row, half step by half step, makes the chromatic scale. Going up, it's usually written with sharps; going down, with flats.

Whole step and half step on the guitar

On the guitar neck the ruler is visual: each fret is a half step. Playing the open E string and then frets 1, 2, 3… you hear E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A… At fret 12 the E comes back, an octave higher.

🎯 Practice Grab your guitar and play the 1st string (high E) open and then at fret 1. Hear how close that is? That's a half step. Now go from open to fret 2: a whole step.
Quick summary
  • Half step = the very next note (1 key, 1 guitar fret).
  • Whole step = 2 half steps.
  • Natural half steps: E–F and B–C.
  • ♯ raises ½ step, ♭ lowers ½ step, ♮ cancels.
  • F♯ and G♭ are the same sound (enharmonic); the name depends on the scale.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a whole step and a half step?

A half step is the smallest distance between two notes (one fret on the guitar, the next key on the piano). A whole step equals two half steps.

Why is there no black key between E and F?

Because the distance between E and F is already just a half step; there's no room for another note. The same happens between B and C.

Is there such a thing as E sharp?

It exists on paper and sounds the same as F. It shows up in scales with lots of sharps, like F♯ major, where each letter has to appear once.
